maybe personal websites should be messy

I've been looking at too many perfectly designed websites lately. Maybe that's the problem.

Published Jul 13, 2026
Reading Time 4 min read
Filed Under #design #thoughts

i've been looking at too many perfectly designed websites lately. everything is the same: a hero section, a grid of cards, a "built with love" footer. somewhere between the gradients and the glassmorphism, all the personality got squeezed out.

i think the problem is that "good design" has become a very specific aesthetic. it has a recognisable language: dark mode, sans-serif, rounded corners, smooth animations, the particular shade of grey that design systems call "muted". and that language is fine, genuinely. but it's become the only language, and when everyone speaks the same language, nobody's saying anything.

the personal websites that have stuck with me over the years don't look like they were made with Figma. they look like someone made them. you can see the decisions: the slightly weird colour palette, the unconventional navigation, the font that doesn't quite fit but somehow works. those imperfections are what make them feel real.

there's a difference between a house that's been lived in and a showroom. both might be technically well-designed. only one feels like a home.

i think about old-school web design sometimes — geocities, angelfire, early forums. the amateur stuff from the early web. it was visually chaotic. but it was also unmistakably personal. someone had sat there and chosen every sparkly gif and animated cursor and garish background. you could tell it was made by a human, and you could usually tell something about what that human was like.

we've made it much harder to make something that feels like that. not technically harder — the tools are better than ever. but aesthetically harder. there's a pressure now to be "professional", to make something that looks like a product, even when you're a person with a blog.

the problem with perfection

i don't think the perfectly designed website is dishonest, exactly. but i do think it creates a kind of distance. everything polished, everything optimised. there's nowhere for the person to leak through.

the interesting stuff about a person — their taste, their preoccupations, the weird specific things they care about — that stuff needs cracks to show through. a perfectly sealed surface doesn't let anything in or out.

this is why i like blogs that have been running for ten or fifteen years and still look kind of the same. you can see the sediment of decisions: the widget added in 2014 that nobody removed, the colour scheme that was very 2012, the sidebar full of links to sites that no longer exist. it's a record. it's evidence that a person has been here for a long time.

what i'm trying to do with this site

i'm trying to make something that feels like it's mine. not minimal in the sense of stripped down to pure function, but minimal in the sense that every element here is something i actually wanted.

i want it to be a little inconsistent, maybe. to feel slightly different between sections because the sections grew at different times. to have the faint impression of choices being made by someone with preferences, not a designer trying to hit a brief.

anyway. if your personal website looks a little weird — doesn't quite follow the rules, has some element that makes no sense to anyone but you — i think that's probably good. hold onto that.
